Key takeaways

  • A banner ad is an image ad shown in a fixed-size space on a website, linked to the advertiser's own page.
  • Banners are best at making new people aware of a business or offer, and at reminding people who already know it.
  • They can be bought by auction (per thousand views or per click) or at a flat rate for a set period.

Banner advertising is one of the oldest and simplest ways to advertise online: you pay to show an image ad in a space on someone else's website, and anyone who clicks it lands on your page. If you have ever seen a wide strip across the top of a news site or a box beside an article, you have seen a banner ad. This guide explains what banner ads are, how they work, the main types and sizes, what they cost and how to run your first one without wasting money.

Banner advertising meaning: a simple definition

A banner ad (also called a web banner or display ad) is a rectangular advertisement placed on a web page. It is usually a still image, sometimes an animated GIF or an HTML5 animation, and it is always linked: clicking it opens the advertiser's website, a product page or a special offer.

Three parties are involved in every banner ad:

  • The advertiser, the business that wants customers and pays for the ad.
  • The publisher, the website owner who sells space on their pages.
  • The visitor, the person reading the page who sees the ad and may click it.

Sometimes they deal with each other directly. More often a platform sits in the middle, such as an ad network that collects space from many sites, or a self-serve marketplace where advertisers book space and upload their banners themselves.

The idea is older than most of today's web. According to Guinness World Records, the first banner ad appeared on 27 October 1994 on HotWired, an early offshoot of Wired magazine. It was an AT&T ad that asked, "Have you ever clicked your mouse right here? You will." It was 468×60 pixels, a size still in use today.

How web banner advertising works

Behind every banner space is a small piece of code that the publisher adds to the page. When a visitor opens the page, that code asks an ad server which banner to show, the server picks one and records an impression (one view). If the visitor clicks, the server records a click and forwards the visitor to the advertiser's chosen page.

Those two numbers give you the most basic measures of a campaign:

  • Impressions: how many times your banner was shown.
  • Clicks: how many people clicked it.
  • Click-through rate (CTR): clicks divided by impressions. Banner CTRs are much lower than search-ad CTRs, which is normal; our guide to banner ad click-through rates explains what to expect.

Some systems also target ads, choosing banners by the visitor's location, the topic of the page or their past browsing. Others simply rotate every eligible banner in a space, so each advertiser gets a fair share of the views.

The main types of banner ads

Banners are mostly grouped by their shape, because the shape decides where on a page they can go:

  • Leaderboards (such as 728×90 and 970×90) run across the top of a page, so they are often the first thing a desktop visitor sees.
  • Rectangles (300×250 and 336×280) sit in sidebars or between paragraphs and work on almost every screen size.
  • Skyscrapers (160×600 and 300×600) are tall and narrow, staying beside the content as people scroll.
  • Mobile banners (320×50 and 320×100) are made for phone screens, usually at the top or bottom of the page.

They can also be grouped by format: static banners (a single image, the simplest and lightest), animated banners (a short sequence of frames) and rich media banners that expand or include video. Static and lightly animated images are accepted almost everywhere, which makes them the easiest place to start. What is banner advertising good for?

Banners reach people while they are reading, not while they are searching. That makes them strongest at jobs that come before a sale:

  • Awareness. Introducing a new business, product or event to people who have not heard of it yet.
  • Promotions. A clear, time-limited offer, such as a seasonal sale or a launch discount, gives people a reason to click now.
  • Staying familiar. Being seen repeatedly on sites your customers read keeps your name in mind for when they are ready to buy.
  • Reaching a niche. A banner on a site about your exact topic puts you in front of an interested audience.

Banners are weaker when someone needs something urgently and goes straight to a search engine. For that moment, search ads usually do better; many businesses use both, as our comparison of banner ads and Google Ads explains.

How much does banner advertising cost?

There is no single price, because banners are sold in different ways:

  • CPM (cost per thousand impressions): you pay each time your banner is shown 1,000 times, usually at a price set by auction.
  • CPC (cost per click): you pay only when someone clicks, again usually set by bidding.
  • Flat rate: you pay one fixed price for a space for a set time, such as a month, however many views or clicks it gets.

Auction prices change with the audience, the targeting you choose, the size and position of the ad and the time of year, so the only reliable way to know your cost is to set a budget and measure what it buys. How to run your first banner ad

  1. Pick one goal and one offer. "Book a free consultation" or "20% off this week" works far better than a general brand message.
  2. Choose a size. If you only make one banner, make it a 300×250 medium rectangle; it fits desktop and mobile pages. Google lists it among its top-performing ad sizes.
  3. Design for a glance. Use a short headline, your logo, one button and strong contrast. Keep the file small: Google Ads, for example, accepts uploaded image ads up to 150 KB.
  4. Send clicks to a matching page. If the banner promises a discount, the page it opens should show that discount straight away.
  5. Tag your link and measure. Add UTM parameters to the link so your analytics shows visits from the banner separately, then judge it on enquiries or sales, not just clicks.
  6. Run it long enough. A full month gives far more reliable results than a few days.

Common banner ad mistakes

  • Too much text. Visitors see a banner for a second or two; a paragraph will not be read.
  • The wrong pixel size. Ad spaces are fixed. A banner a few pixels off is rejected or squeezed.
  • No reason to click. "Welcome to our website" gives people nothing to act on; an offer or a benefit does.
  • Judging by clicks alone. A banner with fewer clicks that brings more customers is the better banner.
  • Misleading content. Fake close buttons, false claims or links that redirect elsewhere get ads removed and damage trust.

Banner advertising: frequently asked questions

What is the difference between banner ads and display ads?

Very little. Display advertising is the broader term for visual ads on websites and apps, and banner ads are its most common form: fixed-size image ads in a space on a page.

Do banner ads still work?

Yes, when they are used for what they do well: making people aware of a business or offer and keeping it familiar. They work best with a clear offer, a matching landing page and results measured in sales or enquiries.

What size should a banner ad be?

The 300×250 medium rectangle is the safest single choice because it fits desktop and mobile pages. The 728×90 leaderboard and 320×50 mobile banner are the next most useful sizes.
