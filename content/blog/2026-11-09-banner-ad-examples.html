Key takeaways

  • The best banners say one thing: a single offer or benefit, readable at a glance.
  • Every good example has three parts: who you are, why it matters now, and one button.
  • Design for the exact size. A tall half page and a 320×50 mobile strip need different layouts, not the same design shrunk.

Looking at banner ad examples is the fastest way to learn what works in a small, fixed space. Below are six original example banners we designed for fictional small businesses, one for each of the most common ad sizes. They are deliberately simple, because simple banners are the ones people actually read. For each banner ad example we explain what makes it work and what you can copy for your own campaign.

All six businesses are invented for this article, so you can borrow the ideas freely. The sizes are the standard ones you will find on most websites; our banner ad sizes chart shows them all side by side.

What every good banner has in common

Before the examples, here is the pattern they all follow. A visitor notices a banner for a second or two, so a banner that works answers three questions instantly:

  1. Who is this? A logo or business name, small but visible.
  2. Why should I care right now? One headline with a clear benefit or offer, ideally with a reason to act soon.
  3. What do I do? One button with an action word: "Shop", "Book", "Claim", "See".

Everything else is optional. Strong contrast, large type and plenty of empty space do more for a banner than extra graphics.

1. Local bakery: 300×250 medium rectangle

Why it works: The headline sells the product's best quality (fresh, every morning), and the smaller line gives a reason to visit this weekend rather than someday. Warm, bread-coloured tones match the product, and the simple loaf illustration is recognisable even at a glance.

Copy this: Pair a timeless benefit with a time-limited offer. The 300×250 is the most widely used size, fitting sidebars and mobile pages alike, so it is the one to make first if you only make one. Google lists it among its top-performing ad sizes.

2. Outdoor store: 728×90 leaderboard

Why it works: A leaderboard is short and wide, so the design reads left to right in one line: logo, offer, button. The discount is in a contrasting colour so the eye lands on it first, and "Sale ends Sunday" adds urgency without shouting.

Copy this: In a wide banner, put the button on the far right, where the reading line ends. Name the product in the button ("Shop jackets") instead of a vague "Click here", so people know what they will see.

3. Plumber: 320×50 mobile banner

Why it works: The 320×50 has room for about five words, so this one uses a question that names the customer's problem and an answer that promises a fast fix. The small trust line ("Licensed local plumbers") and a white button complete it.

Copy this: Write mobile banners from scratch instead of shrinking a desktop design; a shrunk banner is unreadable on a phone. Problem-and-solution headlines suit urgent, local services especially well.

4. Bookkeeper: 160×600 wide skyscraper

Why it works: A skyscraper is read top to bottom, so the order is brand, promise, detail, button. "Tax season, sorted." is short enough to fit the narrow column in large type, and the free review lowers the risk of getting in touch. The rising bar chart hints at growth without needing words.

Copy this: Offer a free first step (a review, a quote, a sample) when your service is something people hesitate to buy from a stranger. Leave empty space in tall banners; it makes the message easier to find.

5. Pet supply shop: 300×600 half page

Why it works: The half page is one of the largest standard sizes, which allows a big, bold headline. Free delivery is a concrete benefit people understand instantly, and the paw print makes the business type obvious before anyone reads a word.

Copy this: Use the extra room for larger type and a simple image, not for more text. A full-width button at the bottom is easy to tap on any screen.

6. Yoga studio: 336×280 large rectangle

Why it works: "Your first class is free" removes the main reason people put off trying a studio. The calm purple gradient and sunrise shape set the mood, while the warm orange button stands out against them.

Copy this: Make the button colour one that appears nowhere else in the banner, so it is the first thing people see after the headline. Speak directly to the reader: "your first class", "claim your".

Banner ad design tips from these examples

  • One message per banner. If you have two offers, make two banners and compare them.
  • Headline first, logo second. People care about the benefit before the brand.
  • Use the exact pixel size. Ad spaces are fixed; a banner a few pixels off is rejected or squeezed.
  • Keep files light. Simple flat designs like these weigh only a few kilobytes. Google Ads accepts uploaded image ads up to 150 KB, a sensible ceiling anywhere.
  • Add a border to light banners. The bakery and bookkeeping banners use a thin border so they do not blend into a white page.
  • Match the landing page. If the banner promises free delivery, the page it opens should say so at the top.
  • Measure, then improve. Track clicks and sales, change one thing at a time, and keep the winner. Our guide to banner ad click-through rates explains what numbers to expect.

Banner ad examples: frequently asked questions

What makes a good banner ad?

A clear headline with one benefit or offer, a visible logo or name, a single button with an action word, strong contrast and the exact pixel size of the ad space.

How much text should a banner ad have?

As little as possible. Aim for a headline of about five to eight words and, on larger sizes, one short supporting line. Small mobile banners have room for only five or six words in total.

Can I use these banner ad examples for my business?

Yes, as inspiration. The businesses are fictional and the designs were made for this article, so feel free to borrow the layouts and ideas with your own brand, offer and colours.

Should banner ads be animated?

They don't need to be. A still banner with a strong message often performs as well as an animated one and loads faster. If you animate, keep it short and end on a frame that works on its own.
